# AnaSOP
Analysis Standard Operating Procedure

## 1. Research Objective

### Candidate Research Directions

The project currently has three related but data-contingent directions.

#### Direction 1: Causal effects of mine clearance

- Question: Does mine clearance improve agricultural production, household consumption,
  children's education, and resilience to climate shocks?
- Intended design: Village-level staggered difference-in-differences and event study based
  on clearance completion dates.
- Current status: Deferred pending access to IMSMA or equivalent task-level land-release
  records. The public baseline survey contains survey dates, not clearance dates, and cannot
  identify the timing of treatment.
- Research value: This is the strongest policy evaluation version of the project and should
  remain the preferred upgrade if restricted administrative data become available.

#### Direction 2: Development costs of persistent mine contamination

- Question: Are villages with more severe mine/ERW contamination disadvantaged in land use,
  agriculture, consumption, food security, and education?
- Intended design: Repeated cross-sectional analysis matching public mine-contamination
  records to household and village outcomes, with local-area and survey-year controls,
  exposure gradients, balance tests, and spatial robustness checks.
- Current status: Feasible in principle with currently available data, subject to verification
  of village matching, common outcome definitions across survey years, and adequate overlap.
- Interpretation limit: This direction estimates the burden associated with conflict legacy
  exposure; it does not by itself identify the causal effect of clearance.

#### Direction 3: Mine contamination and climate resilience — current preferred direction

- Question: Does persistent mine/ERW contamination amplify the adverse effects of floods,
  droughts, or abnormal rainfall on rural livelihoods and human development?
- Intended design: Combine village-level mine exposure with repeated socioeconomic outcomes
  and externally measured climate shocks. Estimate whether otherwise comparable locations
  experience larger losses under the same type of climate shock when mine exposure is higher.
- Current status: Best aligned with currently available public mine data. It does not require
  clearance dates, although it does require acquisition and spatial linkage of public climate
  data.
- Research value: Connects a legacy of armed conflict to present-day adaptation constraints,
  food security, and sustainable development. Direction 2 supplies the baseline effects and
  mechanisms for this main direction.

### Central Research Question

- Research question: Does persistent mine/ERW contamination amplify the adverse effects of
  climate shocks on agricultural livelihoods, household welfare, and children's education in
  rural Cambodia?
- Why it matters: Mine contamination may remain economically consequential even when direct
  casualties are rare. By restricting access to productive land and limiting households'
  adjustment options, it may convert ordinary weather variation into larger and more persistent
  welfare losses.
- Data support currently available: Repeated household surveys contain agricultural production,
  land, consumption, food security, education, migration, and village disaster information.
  Public mine-baseline records contain village identifiers, contaminated locations, land class,
  proximity, perceived risk, operators, and survey dates. CHIRPS monthly rainfall for 2004-2021
  has been acquired and extracted to 2014 commune boundaries.
- What would verify it: A consistent exposure-gradient relationship in which climate shocks
  produce larger declines in agricultural production, consumption, food security, or school
  participation in more heavily contaminated locations; the relationship should survive
  local-area comparisons, flexible geographic controls, alternative exposure definitions,
  and sample-overlap checks.
- What would falsify or weaken it: Poor village linkage, limited overlap between contaminated
  and comparison locations, no consistent interaction between mine exposure and climate shocks,
  effects that disappear after accounting for remoteness and agroecology, or evidence that the
  results are driven by one survey wave or one province.
- Variables likely involved: Mine exposure intensity and proximity; flood, drought, and rainfall
  anomalies; cultivated area, crop production and sales, agricultural inputs, consumption and
  food security, school participation, migration, assets, market access, and household
  demographics.

### Supporting Research Questions

#### Supporting Point 1: Baseline development burden

- Role relative to central point: deepen
- Research question: How strongly is persistent mine/ERW contamination associated with land
  use, agricultural production, consumption, food security, and education?
- Data support currently visible: The socioeconomic surveys contain the relevant household and
  village outcomes, and the public baseline provides several measures of contamination.
- Key variables: Contamination status and intensity, affected land class, proximity, cultivated
  area, crop output, household consumption, food insecurity, and school participation.
- Required feasibility check: Village match rate, geographic coverage, common support, and
  comparability of outcome construction across survey waves.

#### Supporting Point 2: Adaptation mechanisms

- Role relative to central point: mechanism
- Research question: Through which constraints does mine contamination reduce climate
  resilience: restricted land access, weaker agricultural investment, limited market access,
  labor reallocation, migration, or asset depletion?
- Data support currently visible: The surveys include land ownership and use, crop inputs,
  livestock, nonfarm activity, migration, assets, liabilities, infrastructure, and prices, but
  cross-wave consistency remains to be established.
- Key variables: Land use and ownership, input expenditure, crop diversification, livestock,
  nonfarm income, migration, borrowing, durable assets, road and market access, and local prices.
- Required feasibility check: Identify mechanisms measured consistently in enough years and
  distinguish mediators from pre-existing geographic differences.

#### Supporting Point 3: Exposure heterogeneity

- Role relative to central point: heterogeneity
- Research question: Are adverse effects concentrated where contaminated land is agricultural,
  closer to settlements, perceived as more dangerous, or located in poorer and more remote
  communities?
- Data support currently visible: The public mine baseline reports land class, proximity, and
  fear level; the socioeconomic surveys contain household and village characteristics.
- Key variables: Land class, distance or proximity, fear level, contamination area, baseline
  wealth, agricultural dependence, remoteness, and household composition.
- Required feasibility check: Frequency and reliability of each exposure category, spatial
  precision, multiple-testing control, and sufficient observations within local comparison sets.

#### Supporting Point 4: Clearance-data upgrade

- Role relative to central point: broaden
- Research question: If task-level clearance dates become available, does clearance reduce the
  direct development burden of mine exposure and the additional losses caused by climate shocks?
- Data support currently visible: Socioeconomic outcomes are available, but treatment timing is
  not present in the public mine baseline.
- Key variables: Clearance start and completion dates, released area, pre-clearance contamination,
  post-clearance land use, and the same economic and climate outcomes used above.
- Required feasibility check: Administrative-data access, completeness of clearance histories,
  treatment timing relative to survey waves, pre-trend support, and selective prioritization of
  clearance operations.

### Scope of Analysis

- Topics: Post-conflict development, mine/ERW contamination, rural livelihoods, human capital,
  food security, and climate resilience.
- Units of analysis: Household and individual outcomes linked to village-level mine exposure and
  local climate shocks; village-level aggregation where required by linkage or confidentiality.
- Period: Socioeconomic survey waves from 2004 through 2021, with the final estimation window
  determined by village-match quality and the temporal validity of the 2009-2014 mine baseline.
- Geographic scope: Cambodia, with primary attention to rural mine-affected areas and valid
  within-region comparison locations.

### Study Design Declaration

- Research type: applied
- Study design: Repeated cross-sectional observational study with spatial exposure gradients,
  local-area and year controls, and interaction with externally measured climate shocks.
- Current priority: Direction 3 as the central design; Direction 2 as foundational evidence and
  mechanism analysis; Direction 1 as a future administrative-data extension.
- Interpretation limit: Without clearance dates, the project will not claim to estimate the
  causal effect of mine clearance. Climate shocks may provide plausibly external temporal
  variation, but historical mine placement is not random, so the mine-exposure interaction must
  still be interpreted cautiously and tested against geographic confounding.

## 2. Theoretical Background  /  Conceptual Framework  /  Problem Formulation

Research type: applied

### Research Gap

Humanitarian mine action is commonly evaluated through land released, devices destroyed, and
casualties prevented. These outcomes may understate the continuing development burden of
contamination. Mine-affected communities can face restricted access to farmland, infrastructure,
water, forests, and markets; perceived danger can also suppress investment before a casualty
occurs. Less is known about whether these constraints reduce the ability of households to adapt
when floods, droughts, or rainfall anomalies occur. Linking conflict legacy exposure to climate
resilience therefore addresses both a policy gap and a broader sustainable-development question.

### Conceptual Framework

The proposed framework is:

1. Historical conflict creates spatially persistent mine/ERW contamination.
2. Actual and perceived danger restrict access to land and infrastructure and discourage
   agricultural or household investment.
3. These constraints reduce income diversification, asset accumulation, mobility, and access to
   coping mechanisms.
4. When a climate shock occurs, constrained households cannot adjust land, labor, production, or
   consumption as effectively as otherwise comparable households.
5. The resulting losses can appear in agriculture first and subsequently in food security,
   consumption, migration, and children's education.

The main empirical relationship is:

\[
Y_{ivt} = \beta_1 MineExposure_v + \beta_2 ClimateShock_{vt}
          + \beta_3 (MineExposure_v \times ClimateShock_{vt})
          + \alpha_g + \lambda_t + X_{ivt}'\gamma + \epsilon_{ivt}.
\]

Here, \(Y_{ivt}\) is an outcome for household or individual \(i\), village \(v\), and survey
year \(t\); \(\alpha_g\) denotes the most demanding feasible local-area controls;
\(\lambda_t\) denotes survey-year effects; and \(X_{ivt}\) contains pre-specified household and
geographic covariates. The parameter of primary interest is \(\beta_3\), the differential effect
of a climate shock associated with mine exposure.

### Problem Formulation

The immediate problem is not to estimate the treatment effect of clearance. It is to determine
whether a measurable legacy of mine contamination is associated with reduced development and,
more specifically, with greater sensitivity to climate shocks. Direction 3 is preferred because
the public baseline can measure exposure but cannot date treatment. Direction 2 establishes the
direct exposure gradients and mechanisms needed to interpret the resilience result. Direction 1
becomes feasible only if clearance histories can be obtained.

The first feasibility stage must establish: (a) reliable village linkage across administrative
changes and spelling variants; (b) adequate common support within mine-affected regions; (c)
harmonized outcomes across survey waves; (d) climate measures at appropriate spatial and temporal
resolution; and (e) robustness to remoteness, terrain, historical conflict geography, and survey
sampling changes. Failure on these conditions would require narrowing the time period, geography,
outcomes, or strength of the claims.

## 4. Variable Construction  /  Key Variables

### Release Scope and Linkage

The main linked sample uses CSES 2007-2021. CSES 2004 is retained as an unlinked archive and is
not used in the initial mine-linked analysis. The constructed release contains 62,920 main-sample
household-wave observations and person-wave education records linked through household, PSU, and
audited administrative identifiers.

Mine exposure links at village level. The public baseline nonmatch category means **no recorded
point in the public baseline**, not verified mine-free status. The original strict commune-code
climate match is preserved. A separate enhanced climate layer uses exact codes, unique hierarchical
name matches, high-confidence fuzzy commune matches, and explicitly flagged district or province
fallbacks. Of 5,617 PSU-wave rows, 5,574 use commune rainfall, 39 use district rainfall, and 4 use
province rainfall. All enhanced climate links have observed rainfall values.

### Climate Exposure

Rainfall is extracted from CHIRPS for 2004-2021. Polygon-contained grid cells are averaged; small
polygons use the grid cell nearest an interior representative point. Every enhanced link retains
its spatial resolution, method, match score, match margin, grid-cell count, and extraction method.

| variable_name | role | construction_or_coding |
|---|---|---|

| Enhanced Annual Rainfall Anomaly Z | main continuous shock | Rainfall standardized within the same geography over 2004-2021 |

The initial annual construction uses the survey-wave calendar year. Final estimation must compare
it with interview-aligned and preceding-season windows, especially for climate-sensitive
agricultural outcomes.

### Monetary Harmonization

Nominal monetary variables are preserved. Separate real variables are expressed in 2021
constant-price riels using the IMF Consumer Price Index dataflow for Cambodia, whose series are
sourced from national authorities. All-items, food and non-alcoholic beverages, and education CPI
have complete monthly coverage from 2007 through 2021. Each component is rebased to its 2021 annual
mean.

The common conversion is:

\[
RealValue_{it}^{2021}=NominalValue_{it}\frac{CPI_{c,2021}}{CPI_{c,t}}.
\]

Food values use interview-month food CPI. The 2019 wave lacks interview month and therefore uses
the explicitly flagged 2019 annual mean food CPI. Education expenditure uses annual education CPI
because its reference period spans the school year. Agricultural input costs and constructed crop
production value use annual all-items CPI. Physical agricultural outcomes remain primary because a
complete agricultural input or producer-price index is unavailable for the full period.

### Coverage and Interpretation

Direct food-security outcomes are comparable in 2014, 2016, 2017, 2019, and 2021. The ordinal
severity sum is comparable in 2016, 2017, 2019, and 2021. Education attendance is defined for ages
6-17; the comparable education expenditure field begins in 2009. Agricultural area, quantity, and
cost constructions use the stable 2007-2021 fields and units documented above.

### Preprocessing Rules

- No missing values are imputed.
- No variables are winsorized.
- Nonresponse is not converted to zero; observation-count and module-record fields distinguish
  observed zeros from unavailable measures.
- Nominal values, CPI indices, deflators, real values, and deflation-method flags are retained
  together.
- Extreme but source-valid agricultural values remain in the release and require transparent
  influence and functional-form sensitivity checks.
- Original detailed modules remain at their source row grain; concept-level household-wave and
  person-wave files are separate derived layers.
- Strict and enhanced climate links are both retained for sensitivity analysis.
