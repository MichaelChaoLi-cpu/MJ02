## Comment 1

The manuscript identifies regional differences in sensitivity to dry weather but provides little explanation of how agricultural systems reduce or amplify this sensitivity. Please discuss, and where possible analyse, whether differences in cropping systems, irrigation, crop choice, management or other production-system characteristics explain the regional variation in NPP responses. At present, these mechanisms are mostly deferred to future research rather than examined in the analysis.

**Response:**
Thank you for this comment. The revised Methods and Results test how agricultural participation, irrigation coverage, historical road access and crop composition relate to dry-spell sensitivity, with definitions, regional profiles, interaction estimates and sensitivity comparisons in the new Appendix F. The Discussion uses these findings to identify specific production-system conditions for follow-up assessment.

The primary ecological extension jointly interacts consecutive dry days with village agricultural participation, irrigated-land share and historical road distance. Baseline and extended models use the same 41,769 village-year observations from 2,045 villages, retaining village and year fixed effects and region-specific slopes for all four climate variables. The system descriptors summarize observed-period production contexts. Additional specifications allow all climate terms to interact with these descriptors, restrict years to questionnaire releases, or add the crop-composition measures supported by the verified 2019 and 2021 releases.

An interquartile-range higher agricultural participation shifts the NPP response to ten additional dry days by +0.000694 kg C m⁻², whereas an interquartile-range higher irrigated-land share shifts it by −0.001046 kg C m⁻²; both primary interactions have Holm-adjusted p<0.001. Their directions persist when all climate terms interact with the system descriptors, while the road interaction weakens. The crop-composition subset also shows a negative dry-paddy interaction, reported in original units because that share has a zero interquartile range. At common system reference values, R4 and R6 retain negative dry-spell responses, and the six regional slopes remain unequal (p<0.001).

These analyses identify production-system characteristics associated with the spatial variation while retaining the regional contrasts that those characteristics do not fully account for. The Discussion consequently emphasizes seasonal water availability, delivery reliability and crop calendars as concrete assessment priorities, and distinguishes irrigation coverage from the reliability of its buffering performance.

## Comment 2

The household analysis also needs a clearer systems interpretation. The finding that higher NPP is associated with higher food consumption is not that informative by itself. The more important question is why this relationship differs across regions. Please examine or discuss whether the NPP–consumption association varies with agricultural dependence, livelihood diversification, market access, cropping systems, irrigation or other system characteristics. The manuscript mentions some of these possible explanations but does not test them.

**Response:**
Thank you for this comment. The revised household analysis tests production and livelihood characteristics as modifiers of the NPP–consumption association and compares regional slopes before and after each adjustment. The Methods define the measures and comparisons; the Results, Discussion and Appendix F distinguish the regional food-consumption pattern from livelihood-specific contrasts in total consumption.

The four primary modifiers are household agricultural participation, historical road distance, village irrigated-land share and household irrigable-parcel share. Each enters a separate expanded-control regional model and is compared with its baseline on exactly the same households. Rice and dry-paddy composition and explicit nonfarm-business participation provide secondary tests using the verified 2019 and 2021 releases; permanent-market presence provides an exploratory access check. The models retain region and exact interview-year/month effects, released survey weights and village-clustered inference. Holm adjustment applies separately to the four primary and three secondary interactions within each consumption outcome.

For food consumption, none of the primary or secondary interactions meets the Holm-adjusted 5% criterion, and regional slopes remain unequal after every primary system adjustment (all p<0.002). The supporting total-consumption models show a lower NPP slope among agricultural participants (Holm p=0.006) and a higher slope among nonfarm-business participants (secondary-family Holm p=0.044). Thus, the tested livelihood contrasts depend on which consumption dimension is considered; these total-consumption findings are reported separately from the food-consumption result.

The Discussion uses these comparisons to guide region-specific production and livelihood assessment. Appendix F specifies the support and meaning of each indicator, including the distinction between agricultural participation and agricultural income shares, explicit business participation and a complete income portfolio, and historical road distance and current travel costs. This gives the regional interpretation an empirical systems assessment while preserving the food-consumption differences that the individual adjustments do not absorb.

## Comment 3

Please justify the temporal alignment between NPP and household food consumption. Food consumption is measured over a seven-day reference period and then annualised by multiplying by 52, whereas the explanatory variable is annual NPP from the preceding calendar year. It is unclear when during the year the seven-day food-consumption period occurred, whether it corresponds to the relevant agricultural season, or whether seasonal variation in food consumption could affect the estimated relationship. Please explain and, if possible, account for this temporal mismatch.

**Response:**
Thank you for raising this timing issue. The revised Methods distinguish the seven-day recall window from the units used to report consumption, identify actual interview years and months, and justify prior-year NPP as the recent completed-year productivity context. The Results and Appendix E report interview-quarter and annual-lag tests, and the Discussion identifies the regional timing differences that these tests reveal.

Multiplication by 52 changes the reporting units, not the observation window. Each household receives NPP from the calendar year before its actual interview, including interviews that cross calendar years within the 2019 and 2021 releases. Exact interview-year/month effects account for common temporal conditions. Food values use the actual interview-year/month food CPI where available, with an actual-year annual index as fallback.

To address seasonality, the additional models include region-by-interview-quarter level effects and test quarter-specific NPP slopes nationally and within regions, retaining the expanded controls, survey weights and village-clustered inference. Regional food-consumption slopes remain unequal after the seasonal-level adjustment (p<0.001). The national quarterly slope test gives p=0.156, whereas the within-region omnibus test gives p=0.012 and R3 retains evidence after Holm adjustment (p=0.030). Same-household lag comparisons also show a smaller two-year-lag point estimate in R4; the separate intervals are not a formal test of a difference between lag coefficients. These results retain the completed prior-year measure as an annual productivity context while identifying where interview season and the relevant production year require closer attention.

The Methods clarify the measurement, date assignment and additional timing tests:

"Food consumption is constructed from the seven-day recall value multiplied by 52 for reporting in annualized units. The annualized measure retains the original seven-day reference period."
(Page 23, Lines 490–492)

"Household survey-year and survey-month fields determine these assignments where available; the other calendar-year releases use their documented survey year and recorded month. Exact survey-time fixed effects identify each observed combination of interview calendar year and month and absorb common consumption and price conditions at that time. This rule prevents contemporaneous partial-year vegetation conditions from being treated as a completed annual exposure. The completed-year measure provides the recent annual productivity context for the food-consumption snapshot; quarter-specific and annual-lag checks assess whether the relationship depends on interview timing."

## Comment 6

The concepts of exposure, sensitivity and vulnerability are not clearly defined and appear to be used somewhat inconsistently. This is particularly important because established vulnerability frameworks distinguish exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity as related but separate components of vulnerability. Please define these terms explicitly and use them consistently throughout the manuscript, making clear how each concept maps onto the empirical measures used in the study. See for example
Turner, B. L., 2nd, et al. (2003). "A framework for vulnerability analysis in sustainability science." Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A 100(14): 8074-8079. And Urruty, N., et al. (2016). "Stability, robustness, vulnerability and resilience of agricultural systems. A review." Agronomy for sustainable development 36(1): 1-15.

**Response:**
Thank you for highlighting this distinction and recommending these references. The Introduction now explicitly defines exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity and vulnerability, drawing on Turner et al. (2003) and Urruty et al. (2016), and consistently identifies the climate indices as measures of exposure. The Methods clarify how these concepts map onto the empirical analysis: Stage 1 coefficients measure ecological sensitivity, Stage 2 coefficients measure the household economic relevance of productivity, and production and livelihood characteristics can shape sensitivity and adaptive capacity. Vulnerability serves as the overarching conceptual framework. Both recommended references are included in the bibliography. The revised passages are as follows.

"Exposure describes the climatic conditions experienced by an agricultural system, sensitivity describes the magnitude of its response, and adaptive capacity describes its resources and options for adjustment. Vulnerability concerns the system's susceptibility to harm as these dimensions interact (Turner et al., 2003; Urruty et al., 2016). Agricultural climate vulnerability therefore reflects exposure, ecological sensitivity, and the production and livelihood conditions that shape adaptive capacity, rather than a single national exposure statistic (World Bank, 2024)."

The Stage 2 NPP coefficients quantify the household economic relevance of local productivity. Vulnerability provides the conceptual framework; the empirical outputs quantify ecological sensitivity, household economic relevance, and their spatial coincidence."
